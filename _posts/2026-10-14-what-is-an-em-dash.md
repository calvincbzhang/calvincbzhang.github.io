The slide above is from a talk by Terence Tao at ICM 2026, called [Mathematics in the Age of AI](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/slides/age-of-ai-icm-2026.pdf). I like this slide. I find it a little funny, but it also made me think. It seems like using proper punctuation is now sometimes associated with AI. When I see an em dash, I think to myself "oh, this was probably AI-generated!" I even started to remove em dashes from some of my writing to make it look "more human."

The thing is, I think I actually started to use em dashes *because* of AI. I just saw em dashes appear in more and more text, so I kind of just picked up how to use them by example. However, I don't remember ever learning in school how to use one properly. So I thought I should finally learn.

## What is an em dash?

Before looking it up, I thought "--" was an em dash, but that's actually an *en* dash. Turns out the *em* dash is a bit longer. The following table shows the difference between some of the most commonly used horizontal marks.

| Symbol | Name       | Role                                  |
| ------ | ---------- | ------------------------------------- |
| -      | Hyphen     | Joining words, as in *well-made*      |
| −      | Minus sign | Subtraction: 7 − 4 = 3                |
| --     | En dash    | Ranges: *pages 12--18*                |
| ---    | Em dash    | A break or interruption in a sentence |

I also remembered that when you write a paper in LaTeX, you can define spacing in terms of an *em*. It turns out that an em is a unit of typographic measurement. In modern digital typography, one em corresponds to the current type size, e.g., in 12-point type, one em is 12 points.

Traditionally, however, one em was described as the width of a capital M. An en is (typically) half an em (*this sentence is fun to say out loud quickly!*). One useful mnemonic is that the letter `n` only has one bump and the letter `m` has two, so it's double the length (note that this is just a mnemonic that I find useful and not a typographic rule).

## When should I use one?

An em dash is a break that you want to reader to notice, and can also be a substitute for commas, parentheses, a colon, or a semicolon, with some difference in tone.

Let's look at some examples. A pair of em dashes can set off an aside that deserves more attention than it would receive inside commas or parentheses:

> My favorite punctuation mark---which is apparently suspicious now---is the em dash.

A single em dash can introduce an explanation (substituting a colon) or create a sharp turn:

> I had finally learned the rule---then I noticed that different style guides disagree.

It can also mark an interruption or an unfinished thought:

> "But I thought the em dash was---"
>
> "That was an en dash."

The *Chicago Manual of Style* gives a simple piece of advice: [“When in doubt, edit them out.”](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/qanda/data/faq/topics/HyphensEnDashesEmDashes/faq0181.html) So, I guess in my quest to make my text look less AI-generated, I have been following the *Chicago Manual of Style*. I have now learned enough to use em dashes on purpose---which probably means I should use fewer of them.
